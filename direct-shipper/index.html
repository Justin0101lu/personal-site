Three ways to grow, warmest first

Direct Shipper never surfaces a shipper as a target because you hauled it for a broker you still work with. Instead it works outward from what you already have. Receivers — the docks you deliver to weekly, who ship outbound themselves, and where you already have a face and a badge. Dormant relationships — old brokers, with last load date, authority status and your own agreement's clause, so you can see which holds have run their term. Lookalikes — facilities moving freight like yours where you have no relationship at all. On the middle one, Direct Shipper shows dates and documents, never legal conclusions; whether you're clear is a question for your attorney.

Receivers

Docks you already deliver to — and what they ship outbound

The warmest freight you have

You back into these docks every week. Their receiving manager knows your trucks, and no broker put you in that relationship — you earned it on the delivery side. Most of them also ship outbound, and that freight has nothing to do with the broker who tendered you the inbound load. Start here before you cold-call anyone.

Why this works

A broker's non-solicit covers the broker's customer — the shipper who tendered the load. The consignee on the delivery end is usually not that customer, and their outbound freight is their own to award. You're also the only carrier in the conversation who can say "I'm at your dock Thursday anyway." Still read your own agreement: a few are written broadly enough to reach consignees too, and Direct Shipper flags those.

Next time you're there

What this screen is and isn't

Direct Shipper found 9 brokers in your email you haven't hauled for in over a year, including 3 whose operating authority is no longer active. It shows you the last load date, the broker's FMCSA status, and the non-solicit language from the agreement you signed with them. It does not tell you whether you're legally clear. Terms vary, some survive a broker shutting down, and some are assigned to whoever buys the book. Read the clause, then ask your attorney.
